Class Loadouts — Ranger & Priest

Want

  • Power Ranger: Premeditation ×3, Deadly Blow ×2
  • Power Priest: Inspire ×2, Zeal ×2, Call of Power ×1 (SB: Guardian Light Repulse ×1)
  • Removal Ranger: Premeditation ×2, Deadly Blow ×3 (SB: Gate ×1, Doppel ×1, Disturb ×1, Awakened Sundering ×1)
  • Support Priest: Inspire ×2, Call of Power ×2, Zeal ×2, Repulse ×2 (SB: Awakened Dispel ×1, Refuge ×1)

Avoid

  • Ranger: Trap the Damned, Back for Blood
  • Priest: Holy Hymn, Sanctuary, Chosen One — unless it's genuinely your best option (see Ch. V)

5d What if I main Warrior or Mage?

Mage is the guide author's favorite class — but Mage, and to a lesser extent Warrior, pay an opportunity cost simply by not being Ranger or Priest, which limits overall team composition. That's not a flaw in the class. It's the current meta. Almost everything a Warrior can do, a Ranger does better. Mage's Scorch can't reach full potential in a fast, HP-heavy field, and the Shield Ward meta denies its core kit — Petrify, Dispersion Field, and Forgetfulness are dead cards over half the time.

"There's nothing wrong with main'ing as Warrior or Mage and then switching classes just for GSD. I'm a Ranger main, but almost always play Priest in GSD."

— Homelander
✚

What is Support?

Support redirects every resource toward Power — mainly by using Inspire, Zeal, and Call of Power to cut cooldowns and draw cards. Secondarily, Support holds engagement control (EC) and lane control (LC) to protect card advantage: EC shapes the terms battle happens on — Queen Reversed, Awakened Dispel — while LC leans on Repulse and Refuge wherever Removal can't step in.

Support prizes multi-role cards that accelerate their own cooldown — Lovi, Hailey, Jeanne — since the Priest is rarely targeting themselves. It also prizes "amnesia" effects (Rin, Shadowblade) that strip Nimble and open a lane for Ranger removal.

Every diligent Support asks of a new card:

  • How does this build or hold engagement control?
  • How does it accelerate Power?
  • How does it facilitate Removal?

"Bring 3 Inspire, 3 Zeal, and 3 Call of Power and you're already better than 90% of Support players. Don't overthink it."

— LtYurisaki
☍

What is Removal?

Removal clears space for Power to swing and guards Power's "face" — the three field spots directly in front of a hero. Removal owns Damage Mitigation and Lane Control: any card that instantly destroys, removes, or repositions a unit belongs here. It prizes high skill-bounce chances, ejection effects, and — for the same reason as Support — amnesia effects.

A secondary job is suppression and disruption of the enemy Power. Manny the Trickster is a weak form of it; Sow Disorder and Swift Death are solid; Shadow Panda is elite. Neither Removal nor Support usually run pure Power finishers — when Removal takes Shadowblade, it's for the amnesia, not the damage.

Every diligent Removal player asks of a new card:

  • How does this protect my teammates?
  • How does it help me maintain or regain lane control?
  • Have I memorized what this card actually does?

"The two things that matter most as Removal: reading the field fast to see what breaks the enemy, and letting Support see your Deadly Blow countdown. When in doubt, use it — unless it's your last one. Hesitation kills. Shift-click shows your card in chat."

— Rad
⚔

What is Power?

Power's job is to destroy the other team. In the current meta that means magic synergy — global-magic hero damage and cards that grant it to allies — plus the ability to hit face in a single turn, which both damages the enemy hero and denies them summoning room until your unit is dealt with. As the primary target for enemy attacks, Power prioritizes HP first, then Unit Crit, then the Legendary Arena Emblem.

A good Power player:

  • Places cards and picks lanes deliberately
  • Shows cards in chat so Support can plan around them
  • Calls for priority removal actively, not silently
  • Never ends turn before everyone else has
  • Leads the team through the game

"Building a Power deck depends on team synergy. Know your teammates' cards. Keep focusing on those 3–4 cost cards. Unlike Removal and Support, nothing is fixed for you except the goal. Power everyone to victory."

— Homelander

III

Battle Discipline

Rules for the room, not the deck.

06 Don't be a hero

It's a team game with defined roles — everyone gets a chance to shine eventually. High-VIP teams often lose because every player wants to be "the hero" to justify what they spent. Whatever road gets you there, victory always tastes the sweetest.

07 End-turn order matters

Support ends turn first. Neither Power nor Removal should end turn before Support does — Inspire and Zeal can land at any moment. And obviously: auto end turn is always off.

08 He who strikes second, wins

Striking first can win a game. But against evenly matched teams, more often it's the counter-rush that closes it out.

IV

Cost Doctrine

Every card has a countdown (CD) — the cost that decides how fast it hits the field. Reading this scale correctly is, in the guide's words, the difference between a fast deck and a lost game.

1Rare, support-only
2Rare, support-only
3Best cost in the game
4Worst cost in the game
5+Carry at most one

9a 4CD is the worst cost

It carries the highest opportunity cost to play while still being fully vulnerable to Deadly Blow. Unless it has Nimble, a 4-cost card had better be something exceptional — a Leo Incarnation, not a Shan Hai Jin bait card. Check your deck's cost balance: too many 4-cost units means a slow deck, and a slow deck is a lost game. Only Power can run unbalanced 4-cost ratios, since Support's acceleration buffs cover for it. Removal shouldn't run more than two 4-cost cards; Support, no more than three.

9b 5CD+ is too slow

You can't Call of Power → Inspire → play it in the same beat. In a draw-heavy meta, cards need to leave your hand fast — Removal picking targets is the exception. If you do run a 5-cost card as Mage, pair it with a low-cost enabler like Embrave, Inspiration, or Magical Surge.

9c 3CD is the best cost

High enough for real abilities, fast enough to avoid the auto-wipe effects that hit 2-cost and under. The quality of a GSD deck is the quality of its 3-cost cards.

9d 1CD and 2CD can't be frontliners

Beloved as Rick + Alice are, 1- and 2-cost cards are almost always support pieces — the moment a low-cost Power meta emerges, instant-kill answers like Frenzy and Sorann come out to punish it. Good low-cost cards are rare, and there just aren't enough of them to build around.

9e If you must carry a 5CD+, carry exactly one

It takes more than one Inspire to land it, but the upside is you always know exactly what a Call of Power will fetch. Priests should track everyone's highest-cost card and read the field for when it's actually useful.

10 Nids are overrated

The 3-cost Nid is too slow to justify a slot — Jeanne, Starlight, and others do the same job better, or do more. The 1-cost Redstone Nid sees play, but weigh two things: it's only valuable turns 1–2, so you need reshuffle access (mostly a VIP luxury) to reliably draw into it; and if you can't, it's often a dead card from turn 3 onward once negative-HP answers like Ivy, Kristee, or QQ are already on the field.

V

Play What You Have

Not everyone owns whale cards. Do what you can with what you've got.

A Rep-50 Warrior or Rep-50 Mage F2P player is probably going to play Warrior or Mage regardless of the current GSD disadvantages, and that's fine. If you lack defensive tools against whale teams, it may be worth slotting Sanctuary into the Priest's deck anyway. Last week's loss is next week's lesson — it's a process of naming weaknesses and talking them through with teammates. This guild is here to have fun, not to min-max.

VI

Q & A

The questions the guild actually asks, answered without the diplomacy.

But I main Warrior — are you saying I suck?

In GSD, yes. Your best skill, Cull the Weak, can't reliably fire given how common physical deflection is — it's glorious when it lands, but holding it in hand waiting for a Ranger opening is a brutal opportunity cost. Your other strong option, Slash, has no bounce chance, so it leans on whale-tier graveyard cycling like Golden Dawn Reversed. Adapt toward Power or Removal; the meta is what it is.

But I main Mage — same question?

Also yes, and the "mercy" cards the devs added recently suck too. Mage's strengths — skill cycling, skill bounce, slowing the game down — shine in League, not in a timed mode like GSD, where cycling past turn 4 gets punished hard by auto-distract effects (World Reversed, Chu) or auto-damage (QQ). Forgetfulness, Petrify, and Charm do nothing in a Queen Reversed meta. Purge is about the best you've got, which puts Mage a notch below even Warrior. Adapt toward Power or Support.

I'm a dev with a secret alt in your guild and I ruined two of the four classes chasing revenue. How do I fix it?

We both know you're happy with how things are and have no real interest in fixing it. There's always a sequel where you can do it all over again.

I don't like the magic-synergy meta — it feels cheap.

Devs have tried to answer it twice: Somnambulist, which nobody runs, and Envoy, which doesn't respawn on death like Oath and has far too little HP. In practice, people lean on Oathshield plus infinite graveyard respawn — powerful when it's set up, but fragile and easily disrupted by any team that comes prepared with temptation or discard damage. The newest attempt is the Thunder Devourer tower. Still shaking out.

People run counter-meta tech like Kirin — what do you think?

Seeing a Kirin on the field usually says the player didn't have a better card to run — which usually means they were going to lose anyway. Kirin, Chu, and World Reversed all assume the meta is skill spam. It isn't. The major defensive meta is Awakened Queen Reversed; the minor is loop-spawned Oathshield. The major offensive meta is magic synergy; the minor is blocking summon squares. Skill spam can accelerate those patterns, but stopping skill spam doesn't stop them. Run the best cards for your matchup — just don't mistake the symptom for the trend.

Why do Power, Support, and Removal have different max card counts?

Different roles accelerate at different rates. Rangers, thanks to Premeditation, draw faster than anyone else, so a well-built Ranger deck plays cards quicker and can afford to carry more of them. For Power specifically, going over 15 cards lowers the odds of drawing your best cards on any given turn — fewer is more.

What do "face" and "ejection" actually mean?

Face is the three field spots directly in front of a hero. Ejection is a removal effect that yeets a card off the field entirely — Scary, Fear, Dismiss, and Alice's Awakening all work this way.

How do team rosters actually get decided?

The guild qualifies for a maximum of 3 teams of 4 (12 players) based on weekly contribution, tallied every Friday — 30,000 contrib by Friday evening is the bar for a shot at three teams, and only the top two guilds by that week's contribution can field three. Contribution after Friday matters far less. Rewards from GSD itself require a minimum of 700 contrib by Friday.

Roster spots come down to four factors, roughly in this order of leverage:

  • Did you confirm availability with Home, Rad, or Nem — ideally Friday evening through Saturday night?
  • Does this exact team combination look likely to win? A track record of winning overrides almost everything else.
  • Are you new and above level 40? New players get priority for a few weeks to help their Guild Research along.
  • What was your contribution this week? Higher contrib, higher odds — in an ideal world this would be the only factor.
How do you decide who plays with whom?

Regular pairings are honored where possible — knowing your usual teammates lets everyone tune decks and card-buying goals together, and it tends to be more fun both to play and to watch. The four factors above still come first, so it isn't guaranteed every week.

What about your own seat?

Team 1 isn't the author's preference — it's stressful, and protecting Homelander as Removal is a lot of responsibility. The 4th seat is more fun, so that's usually where he slots himself. He soloed Team 2 back when the guild had only 5–6 GSD regulars, over a year and a half ago — the "dark days" that only a handful of long-timers (ggfa, hieu, Mim, Edel, Noot) still remember.

What should we expect from GSD going forward?

The meta always shifts. Complaints about a spike meta produced a counter-rush nimble meta; complaints about that produced a wave of amnesia/forgetfulness/charm play, which hardened into today's Shield Ward meta. The current magic-synergy offense is already drawing "synergy OP" complaints and answers like the Thunder Devourer wall — with more elemental walls likely to follow. Expect another swing where old cards resurface, Warrior and Mage possibly rebound, and today's "OP" cards get downgraded to "meh." Adjust accordingly.
